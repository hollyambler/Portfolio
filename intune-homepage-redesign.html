Case Study · Microsoft Intune · 2024

Redesigning the Intune admin homepage

How four rounds of design review with enterprise admins reframed a homepage redesign from "make it look coherent" into a question of trust, monitoring, and data customers can actually rely on.

My role

Senior UX Researcher — sole researcher, end to end

Methods

Iterative design reviews across four sessions

Participants

Enterprise IT admins from the Intune Customer Connection Panel

Impact

Shifted the team from a curated homepage to a customizable card gallery

Context

A redesign that started as a coherence project

Microsoft Intune is the endpoint-management product IT administrators use to secure and manage the devices, apps, and policies across their organizations. One of the Intune team's ongoing objectives was visual and interaction coherence with the other portals in the Microsoft Security suite — aligning the Intune admin homepage with the shared Security Fluent Extension (SFE) design system.

The product team had four clear goals for the redesigned homepage: display dynamic data tailored to each customer's usage, surface quick links to frequently used objects, let customers customize parts of the experience, and — most importantly — craft a homepage customers would actually value and return to.

That last goal was the tension. Because in its current state, the homepage was something customers actively avoided.

“It's something I skip over. It's the first thing that loads, and the first thing I skip away from to do work.”

Enterprise IT administrator · Study participant

I was brought in to run design reviews on a set of SFE-aligned homepage proposals — to collect feedback and, more fundamentally, to capture what admins genuinely need and want from the homepage experience. What the research surfaced went well beyond visual polish.

Approach

Iterative design reviews with real admins

I ran the study as a series of moderated design reviews with enterprise IT administrators recruited through the Intune Customer Connection Panel — practitioners who live in the product every day. Rather than a single round, I structured the work across four sessions, so that what I learned in one review sharpened the questions and stimuli in the next.

In each session I walked participants through a homepage mockup built on SFE patterns and probed not just for reactions to the layout, but for the underlying job they were trying to do when they landed on the page. I paid particular attention to where their described behavior diverged from the design's assumptions — the gap between what the page offered and what admins actually opened the product to accomplish.

All participant names, quotes attributions, organizational details, and unreleased visual designs have been redacted or generalized for this public case study. Quotes are lightly edited for length and clarity.
What the mockup assumed

Reading the design against the job

The mockup I tested followed SFE conventions faithfully. At the top — the prime real estate — sat a sizable hero banner. Beneath it was an area for pinning shortcuts to frequently used objects. Data visualizations lived below the fold, and the design replaced links to the Support blog with a curated news feed.

Session by session, participants told me the priority order was almost exactly inverted from what the layout implied. I mapped their reactions region by region:

What the design put first

  • A large hero banner — read by admins as marketing — in the top slot
  • "Pick up where you left off" content
  • Pinned shortcuts as a secondary element
  • A curated news feed replacing the Support blog
  • Data visualizations placed below the fold

What admins actually wanted first

  • Actionable data visualizations — the single most valuable thing to surface
  • The hero area repurposed for feature alerts, not marketing
  • Their own pinned shortcuts over generic "pick up where you left off"
  • Direct links to the Support blog over a news feed
  • The ability to choose which cards appear at all

The most valuable region of the page was the one placed below the fold. The prime real estate was spent on the content admins trusted least.

Findings

Four things customers need from the homepage

01

Accurate data visualizations are the whole point

Actionable, trustworthy charts are the most valuable thing the homepage can surface. Ideally admins could choose which to display — including their own queries from Power BI, Log Analytics, and Graph.

02

Customization beats curation

Across all four sessions, participants preferred a dashboard they control over a Microsoft-curated homepage — because every organization, tenant, and administrator is different.

03

Data needs context to be trusted

Numbers need timestamps, a time scale, and counts framed as fractions. Data also has to refresh often enough — or let admins set their own thresholds — to feel current rather than stale.

04

Marketing content erodes trust

Promotional content in the homepage's most prominent space actively lowered admins' confidence in the page — the opposite of the goal of making it valuable.

The core insight

The homepage didn't support the job admins came to do

The consensus across every session was blunt: the homepage wasn't valuable because it didn't support Monitoring — continuously observing signals about device, app, and system health so end users can securely get to the resources they need. Monitoring is the admin's primary Job-to-be-Done, and the homepage simply didn't serve it.

That's why admins skipped past it to go straight to their workloads and individual objects. The redesign, as scoped, risked making a prettier version of a page people already ignored. Several participants said as much — and pointed to where the team's effort would actually pay off.

“If you're showing me a card with data and time frames that don't matter to me, why would I ever look at it? There's a split between trying to make the homepage a pseudo-dashboard, and I'd much rather see you work on the actual dashboard.”

Enterprise IT administrator · Study participant

“I'm looking at these metrics, but not in Intune. I'm using Graph, Log Analytics, Power BI. I'd LOVE to pull the work I've done outside of Intune into Intune and have that single pane of glass. That would be amazing.”

Enterprise IT administrator · Study participant

The desire was consistent: not more curated content, but a single pane of glass the admin assembles themselves — pulling in the monitoring signals they already rely on, from wherever those signals live.

Depth: why framing matters

The difference between a number and a signal

One of the sharpest threads in the research was how easily a data card can mislead. Admins didn't just want charts — they wanted charts framed so the severity is unmistakable at a glance. A raw count or a percentage can hide whether something is routine or on fire.

The same failure, framed two ways — as participants described it
32% failed
A percentage with no denominator and no timestamp. Is that 20 of 500,000, or 20 of 20?
Ambiguous — can't act on it
8 of 25
App installs failed in the last 24 hours · updated 11:40 AM
Concrete — severity is obvious

“App install failure: 20 out of 500k is a low failure rate, but 20 out of 20 is serious — and that's not conveyed by this card.”

Enterprise IT administrator · Study participant

The same concern extended to freshness. Admins push policies and enroll devices in real time; a card that silently shows data from an unknown window is worse than no card at all, because it invites a false sense of safety.

“Is it including anything older than 24 hours, or only in that 24-hour window? People could miss two days of unhealthy status.”

Enterprise IT administrator · Study participant

These are small design details with outsized trust consequences. They became concrete, testable requirements: show the denominator, show the timestamp, show the time scale, and make the refresh cadence visible or configurable.

Recommendation

From a curated homepage to a card gallery

My central recommendation was to stop treating the homepage as something Microsoft curates for the admin, and start treating it as something the admin assembles. Concretely: let customers choose from a gallery of cards to build their own homepage — balancing the genuinely desirable elements of a full dashboard against the team's homepage goals, rather than forcing a choice between them.

Alongside that, I recommended reclaiming the hero banner for feature alerts instead of marketing, keeping direct Support-blog links over a news feed, and — underpinning all of it — a set of data-integrity rules every card must follow so the metrics feel current and trustworthy.

Impact

The research reframed the team's understanding of the problem and changed what they prioritized:

  • Reframed the brief. A "make it coherent with SFE" visual project became a conversation about whether the homepage should support Monitoring — the admin's real job — at all.
  • Redirected the roadmap conversation toward a customizable card gallery and the underlying dashboard, and away from investing further in static, curated content admins had told us they skip.
  • Produced concrete, testable design requirements for data cards — denominators, timestamps, time scales, and refresh cadence — that any future homepage or dashboard card could be held to.
  • Gave the team language for the north star customers kept describing: a single pane of glass admins assemble from the signals they already trust.
Reflection

What I'd carry forward

The most useful thing this study did was resist the framing it was handed. The request was to evaluate a layout; the honest finding was that the layout question was downstream of a much bigger one about what the homepage is for. Running the reviews iteratively across four sessions is what gave me the confidence to say that — I wasn't reacting to one strong opinion, I was watching the same conclusion form independently, session after session.

If I were extending this work, I'd move next to prototyping the card gallery and testing the data-integrity rules directly — putting a "8 of 25, updated 11:40 AM" card in front of admins against a "32%" card and measuring the difference in confidence and action, rather than only hearing it described.
